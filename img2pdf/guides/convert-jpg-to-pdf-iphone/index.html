How to Convert JPG to PDF on iPhone: 3 Free Built-in Methods

Converting JPG images into PDF format is one of the most frequent document tasks on iPhone. Whether you are packaging a receipt for expense reimbursement, preparing medical records, or sending signed paperwork to an office, sending a standardized PDF is far more reliable and professional than emailing raw image files.

Why Convert JPG to PDF?

JPG and JPEG files are designed for digital photos. While they display clearly on screens, they present several problems when used for business and official documentation:

  • Inconsistent printing and scaling: Different devices, email clients, and operating systems render JPG dimensions differently, resulting in cropped edges or unpredictable print sizes.
  • Lack of multi-page bundling: If a document spans three pages, sending three loose JPG attachments forces the recipient to open each image individually rather than reading a single continuous file.
  • No text selection: Standard JPG images contain raw pixels without an underlying text layer, making it impossible for recipients to search, copy, or index important details.

Packaging your JPGs into a standardized PDF resolves these issues. Here are the three completely free built-in methods available directly in iOS, followed by an on-device utility for advanced document control.

Method 1: Apple Files App Quick Action

If your JPG files are saved in the Files app (or you save them from Photos or Safari into Files), iOS provides a direct Quick Action that generates a PDF in seconds. Apple details folder navigation in Use the Files app on iPhone.

  1. Open the Files app on your iPhone.
  2. Browse to the folder containing your JPG images.
  3. Tap the More icon (circle with three dots) in the upper right corner and choose Select.
  4. Tap the JPG file you want to convert (or select several files if you want them combined).
  5. Tap the More button in the bottom-right toolbar.
  6. Tap Create PDF.

A new PDF file will appear immediately in the same folder bearing the original image name. It costs nothing, requires no internet connection, and never adds watermarks.

Method 2: Photos App Print-to-PDF Gesture

If your JPG photos are stored in the Apple Photos camera roll, you do not even need to export them to Files first. You can use iOS's built-in Print preview gesture. Apple describes iOS printing functionality in Print from iPhone.

  1. Launch the Photos app and locate your JPG image.
  2. Tap the Share button in the lower-left corner.
  3. Scroll down through the action list and select Print.
  4. On the Print Options screen, ignore printer selection. Look at the visual preview at the bottom.
  5. Place two fingers on the page preview thumbnail and perform a pinch-out gesture (zoom outward).
  6. The preview instantly transforms into a system PDF view.
  7. Tap the Share button on this screen and choose Save to Files, Mail, or Messages.

This conversion runs entirely inside Apple's native print engine. No external services are contacted.

Method 3: Apple Shortcuts Automation

For users who regularly convert photos, Apple provides the Shortcuts app to build automated workflows. Apple's guide for creating automations is available at Shortcuts User Guide for iPhone.

  1. Open the Shortcuts app on your iPhone.
  2. Tap the plus icon (+) to create a new shortcut.
  3. Add the Select Photos action and ensure multi-selection is enabled.
  4. Add the Make PDF action immediately beneath it.
  5. Add a final action such as Share or Save File.
  6. Name the shortcut "Make PDF" and add it to your Home Screen or Share Sheet.

Once created, this shortcut gives you a one-tap conversion button right from your Home Screen.

Limitations of Built-in Methods

While Apple's native tools are handy, they were created for general convenience rather than rigorous document workflows. Common issues include:

  • Unpredictable page sequencing: When selecting multiple JPGs in Files, iOS orders them strictly by file name or date. There is no drag-and-drop thumbnail view to check or reorganize pages.
  • Massive PDF file size: A 12MP or 48MP iPhone photo converted straight to PDF can easily produce a 15MB to 30MB file. Email providers typically cap attachments at 20MB or 25MB, causing delivery failures.
  • No paper size selection: Built-in conversion defaults to matching the photo's original pixel dimensions rather than standard US Letter (8.5x11 inches) or A4 (210x297mm) paper sheets.
  • Absence of text recognition (OCR): Built-in tools do not embed searchable OCR text layers into the resulting PDF.
